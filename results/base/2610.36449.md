**Curator's take**  
The paper asks whether vision‑language models (VLMs) can use visual symbols to communicate, and whether they interpret such symbols like humans do. The authors, a team from Google DeepMind and Stanford, find that simple visual cues (e.g., a red heart) can steer VLM behavior on unrelated tasks, but the models’ verbal explanations of this influence are weak. They also show that VLMs can generate creative visualizations to convey abstract ideas, yet these visual messages often fail to be interpreted correctly by other models or by humans.

**Key points**

- **Steering effect** – Simple visual symbols such as a red heart can strongly influence VLM behavior on unrelated tasks, steering models toward recursive solutions on simple coding problems. This steering is largely implicit; models’ direct interpretations and thinking traces about visual symbols diverge substantially from their observed behavior.

- **Generation–interpretation gap** – In visual communication between models, the generation–interpretation gap is pronounced: models tend to send shape primitives that they (and humans) find intuitive, but these visual cues often fail to steer other models. The models’ own visualizations are not always interpreted in the intended way.

- **Creative visual communication** – In further communication games involving abstract concepts and stories, models can creatively use visuals to transmit meaning to one another, although some communications remain non‑intuitive to humans. The visualizations that prove most effective for steering or that emerge across multi‑turn communication games are often less legible to humans.

- **Alignment with humans** – Models’ visual meaning spaces are only partially aligned with human interpretations. The visualizations that are most effective for steering or that emerge in multi‑turn games are often less legible to humans, highlighting a divergence between model and human visual conventions.
